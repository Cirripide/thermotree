About Thermotree

This map shows where a city is hot and where it is green, neighborhood by neighborhood, computed from satellite imagery. Pick any city in the world and a summer year, and the map redraws for that place.

It opens by default on the Where Trees Are Needed view: the spots that are both hot and short on vegetation, where new trees would do the most good.

Why this project

Cities trap heat. Asphalt and concrete soak up the sun all day and release it slowly into the night, while trees and grass do the opposite. As the climate warms, urban heat becomes a growing health issue, especially for older residents and people who work outdoors.

The goal is to raise awareness of the need to plant more trees in cities, and to give an initial indication of which areas need them most: the cells that come out both very hot and very low on vegetation. Those are where new trees, parks, or green roofs would have the largest cooling impact, and where the conversation about urban greening should start.

The three views

Switch between them with the Map view control in the sidebar:

  • Where Trees Are Needed (the default). Only the priority cells are shown, in solid red: the areas that are both hot and low on vegetation at the same time, where planting would do the most to cool the city. Everywhere else is left as plain map.
  • Heat vs Vegetation. The full picture, split by a draggable slider. On the left, heat: bright yellow is hot, dark purple is cool. On the right, vegetation: dark green is dense and leafy, grey is bare or built up.
  • Show basemap. Hides the colors and leaves just the street map and the grid, for orientation.

How to read the map

The city is split into a uniform grid of 200 × 200 m cells. The values are surface temperature and greenness measured by satellite (rooftops, asphalt, parks, water), not the air temperature you would read on a weather station.

Cells drawn with diagonal stripes have no data for that summer. This happens when the satellite could not get a clean read of that spot across the three summer months: too much cloud cover, too few clear passes, or no consistent signal over June, July and August.

Use the Summer dropdown to compare years. That is where lasting change shows up, like a new park or a new development, rather than the noise of a single summer's weather.

Where the data comes from

  • Heat: NASA and USGS Landsat 8 and 9 satellites, Surface Temperature product (Collection 2, Level 2). 100 m thermal sensor, daytime passes only.
  • Vegetation: ESA Sentinel 2 satellites, surface reflectance product. 10 m red and near infrared bands.
  • City boundary: OpenStreetMap (via Nominatim).

For each summer (June, July and August), the app combines every clear satellite pass over the city into a single median composite, which filters out cloudy days, smoke, and short lived anomalies. The composite is then averaged inside each 200 m cell.
